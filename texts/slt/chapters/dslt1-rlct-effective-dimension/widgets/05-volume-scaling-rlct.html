The RLCT $\lambda$ as a volume-scaling exponent

Watanabe defines the Real Log Canonical Threshold as the scaling exponent of the volume of "almost true" parameters near a singularity: $$ V(\varepsilon) \;=\; \int_{\{w \in W \,:\, K(w) < \varepsilon\}} \varphi(w)\, dw \;\;\sim\;\; c\, \varepsilon^{\lambda}\quad\text{as } \varepsilon \to 0. $$ Plot $\log V(\varepsilon)$ against $\log \varepsilon$, read off the slope at small $\varepsilon$, and that is $\lambda$. The "effective dimension" of the model at the singularity is $2\lambda$ — for regular models $\lambda = d/2$ exactly, but for singular models $\lambda$ can be a fraction: the famous "half a dimension" of $K = w^4$.

Recognise mode: pick a $K$, predict $\lambda$, then watch the slope confirm. Construct mode: drag $k$ in $K(w) = w^{2k}$ to match a target effective dimension — rational targets like $2\lambda = 1/3$ pin down $k$ exactly.

When $k$ is non-integer the formula $K(w) = w^{2k}$ technically isn't a polynomial; we plot it anyway because the volume integral still has a power-law slope $1/(2k)$. Only integer $k$ corresponds to an actual polynomial loss landscape; e.g. $k = 3$ gives $K = w^6$.

How $V(\varepsilon)$ is computed. For 1D $K$: a 4001-point uniform grid on $[-1, 1]$ is used; $V(\varepsilon) = (\#\{w_i : K(w_i) < \varepsilon\}) \cdot \Delta w$. For 2D $K$: a $200 \times 200$ uniform grid on $[-1, 1]^2$, same idea. The slope $\lambda$ is fitted by least squares on the smallest-$\varepsilon$ decade where the asymptotic $V \propto \varepsilon^\lambda$ has time to dominate. For multi-singularity $K$ like $K = (w+1)^2 (w-1)^4$, only the global RLCT (the smaller of the local $\lambda_{c_j} = 1/(2k_j)$) shows in the slope, because the largest singularity dominates the integral as $\varepsilon \to 0$. The 2D normal-crossing case picks up a $(\log 1/\varepsilon)^{m-1}$ multiplicity factor when several $k_j$ tie for the minimum — that's why the K = ½ w₁² w₂² log-log plot has a slight visible curvature even though the asymptotic slope is exactly $\lambda = 1/2$.
