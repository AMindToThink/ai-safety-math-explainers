In a Bayesian setting the posterior over parameters is $$ p(w \mid D_n) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z_n}\, \varphi(w)\, e^{-n L_n(w)} \;\propto\; \varphi(w)\, e^{-n L_n(w)}, $$ and as $n \to \infty$ this is dominated by the loss landscape $K(w)$ (the limit of $L_n$): $p(w \mid D_n) \,\propto\, \varphi(w)\, e^{-n K(w)}$.

For a regular model $K$ has a single non-degenerate minimum, so the posterior tightens to a multivariate Gaussian with covariance $\frac{1}{n}\, I^{-1}$ — asymptotic normality. For a singular model the picture is funkier: the posterior trails along the variety $W_0$, splits unevenly between competing singularities, and (in general) is not Gaussian even at $n = 10^6$. This widget lets you see that funkiness directly: pick a $K$, drag $n$, watch the posterior.

How this widget computes the posterior. For each preset, the unnormalised posterior $\varphi(w)\, e^{-n K(w)}$ is evaluated on a grid (401 points in 1D; 100×100 in 2D), with $\varphi$ taken uniform on the plot window and $K$ taken in the large-$n$ approximation $L_n \approx K$. Numerical stability uses the standard log-sum-exp trick — without it, $e^{-100 \cdot 5}$ underflows. In 1D the curve is the true probability density (mass-normalised over the plot window), so the y-axis shows real probability density and peak heights scale with $n$ — that scaling is itself part of the lesson. In 2D the heatmap is normalised to its peak before colouring (with a square-root contrast so low-density structure stays visible) so what you see is shape, not absolute density. The W₀ overlay and toy K family are shared with widget 1 (01-bayes-loss-landscape).
