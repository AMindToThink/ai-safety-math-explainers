Theorem 4.1.1 (Convergence). For any logical inductor $\mathbb{P}$ and any sentence $\varphi$, the limit $\mathbb{P}_\infty(\varphi) := \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}_n(\varphi)$ exists.

Why? Because if $\mathbb{P}_n(\varphi)$ failed to converge, it would oscillate above $p + \varepsilon$ and below $p - \varepsilon$ infinitely often — and a trader who buys low at $p - \varepsilon$ and sells high at $p + \varepsilon$ would book $\approx 2\varepsilon$ per cycle, forever. The trader exploits the market, contradicting the LI criterion. So a non-convergent price cannot belong to an LI.

Market price $\mathbb{P}_n(\varphi)$ over days $1, \ldots, 100$. Green dots = days the trader buys (price below $p - \varepsilon$). Red dots = days the trader sells (price above $p + \varepsilon$).

Trader's realised cumulative cash from completed buy/sell cycles. Each completed cycle adds $\approx 2\varepsilon$ — and there is no plausible world in which this cash drops below the realised running total.

Why this argument is the template

Every property in §4 follows this same shape:

  1. Suppose the property fails by some $\varepsilon$.
  2. Build an explicit trader that, exploits this failure, profiting $\approx \varepsilon$ per opportunity.
  3. Conclude by the criterion that the property must hold.

The convergence argument is the cleanest example: the trader is just buy low, sell high. Limit coherence (Theorem 4.1.2) is the same trick applied to mutually exclusive sentences. Provability induction (Theorem 4.2.1) is the same trick applied to an EC sequence of theorems. The widget's worth-plot is the universal "see the contradiction" panel.

"A trader can wait until $\mathbb{P}_n(\varphi) < p - \varepsilon$ and then buy a share in $\varphi$ at the low market price… and then sells back the share in $\varphi$ at the higher price. This trader makes a total profit of $2\varepsilon$ every time $\mathbb{P}_n(\varphi)$ oscillates in this way, at no risk, and therefore exploits $\mathbb{P}$." — Garrabrant et al. (2016), §4.1

What the model is and isn't. The trader sketched here uses a discontinuous threshold rule (buy when price strictly below $p - \varepsilon$, sell when strictly above). Per the §3.4 continuity constraint, an actual LI-exploiting trader has to be continuous; the proof sketch in §4.1 explicitly notes "the trader we have sketched makes use of discontinuous trading functions, and so is not a well-formed trader. These details are treated in §4.1." The intuition of "buy at the trough, sell at the peak" is exactly the right one; the technical version uses a continuous indicator function (see ctsind in the paper) that gives ${\sim}1$ in the buy zone, ${\sim}{-1}$ in the sell zone, and ramps continuously between. A trader using such an indicator captures essentially the same per-cycle profit as the discontinuous one — see module 3 of Ch 2 for the continuity story.
