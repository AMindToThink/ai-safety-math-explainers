Pick a digit position $n$. Let $\varphi := \quot{\pi[n] = 7}$. Either $\pi[n] = 7$ or it isn't — in standard mathematics this is a fixed truth, not a chance event. But for any reasoner who hasn't computed $\pi[n]$, the truth value is logically uncertain. What probability should they assign?

A perfect Bayesian, by the laws of probability theory, is forced into an answer that is plainly absurd. Watch.

Why the Bayesian is forced into 100%

Two preliminaries from probability theory and logic:

  1. If $A \Rightarrow B$, then $P(A) \le P(B)$. (This is one of the Kolmogorov axioms applied to monotonicity of measure: $A \Rightarrow B$ means $A \subseteq B$ as events, and measures of subsets are bounded by measures of supersets.)
  2. If $A$ is a tautology — provable from no assumptions — then $P(A) = 1$.

Now suppose $\pi[n] = 7$ is in fact true. Then so is the conjunction $\quot{1+1=2 \;\land\; \pi[n] = 7}$. And in propositional logic,

$\quot{1+1=2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \quot{1+1=2 \;\land\; \pi[n] = 7} \;\Longrightarrow\; \quot{\pi[n] = 7}$.

The first implication holds because conjoining a true statement to a true statement preserves truth. The second is just $\land$-elimination. Chaining (1) and (2):

$1 = P\bigl(\quot{1+1=2}\bigr) \;\le\; P(\varphi)$.

So $P(\varphi) \ge 1$. Probabilities are bounded by 1, hence $P(\varphi) = 1$. A perfect Bayesian who has never computed $\pi[n]$ is nonetheless forced to be 100% sure that $\pi[n] = 7$. But this only holds if $\pi[n] = 7$ in fact; if $\pi[n] \ne 7$, the same argument forces $P(\varphi) = 0$. The Bayesian's probability is *forced* to track the truth — meaning probability theory cannot represent any state of *uncertainty* about $\varphi$.

"In any given practical scenario, reasoners usually experience a mix of both empirical uncertainty (about how the world is) and logical uncertainty (about what that implies). In this paper, we focus entirely on the problem of managing logical uncertainty. Probability theory does not address this problem, because probability-theoretic reasoners cannot possess uncertainty about logical facts." — Garrabrant et al. (2016), §1

What a logical inductor does instead

A logical inductor's price $\mathbb{P}_t(\varphi)$ on day $t$ is not constrained by $\varphi$'s eventual truth — it is constrained by the inability of any polynomial-time trader to exploit $\mathbb{P}$. In particular:

  • If digits of $\pi$ at position $n$ are pseudorandom relative to all polynomial-time predictors, the inductor's price for $\quot{\pi[n] = 7}$ converges to $\approx 0.1$ — the empirical-frequency prior — driven by traders who profit whenever the price drifts away from $0.1$. The prior is not hardcoded; it emerges from the trading dynamics. (See Flint's uniform_digits_of_pi.py for a runnable instance: a single s-curve trader with intercept $0.1$, and the credence climbs/oscillates toward $0.1$ over many update steps rather than starting there.)
  • Once the deductive process emits the resolved value, the price snaps to 0 or 1 in a timely manner — again driven by traders, this time exploiting the gap between the now-known truth and any non-trivial price.
  • Crucially, before resolution the inductor's price can represent uncertainty: $\mathbb{P}_t(\varphi) \approx 0.1$ is allowed, even though by ground truth either $\varphi$ or $\lnot \varphi$ is provable.

This is what §4.3 of the paper formalizes as the Statistical-Pattern Learning theorem. The "10%" estimate is the right answer in the same sense that "the probability the next coin flip is heads given a fair coin is 50%" is the right answer — even though for any single flip there's a ground truth.

How this widget thinks about the LI's price. The widget's "logical inductor" is a stand-in: a flat $0.1$ line that snaps to $0$ or $1$ on day $\tau(n)$, where $\tau(n) = \lceil n / 5 \rceil + 5$. This is a heuristic for a true LI's resolution day — the day the deductive process $D_n$ first emits $\varphi$ or $\lnot \varphi$ — and the n-dependent shape is purely cosmetic, not a property of any real LI. The flat $0.1$ before resolution is also a simplification: in the actual LIA of §5, the price converges to $\approx 0.1$ as s-curve traders (sigmoids centred at $0.1$, slope $\sim 10$) push the price toward the empirical-frequency prior — see Flint's uniform_digits_of_pi.py, which is small enough to run in seconds and shows the convergence explicitly. The qualitative trajectory shown here — flat prior then snap to resolution — is correct; the "exactly $0.1$ from day 1" detail is not. The Bayesian's "price" is shown as $1$ throughout if $\pi[n] = 7$ in our pre-computed first-100-digit table; otherwise $0$. Either way, it never floats — that's the whole point.
