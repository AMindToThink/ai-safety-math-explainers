A deductive process $\bar{D}: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathrm{Fin}(\mathcal{S})$ is a computable nested sequence $\bar{D}_1 \subseteq \bar{D}_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of finite sets of sentences — think of it as a slow oracle that emits theorems one day at a time.

A world is any truth assignment $W : \mathcal{S} \to \{0, 1\}$. The set $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_n)$ — worlds propositionally consistent with $\bar{D}_n$ — is the set of truth assignments that satisfy every sentence emitted so far. As $n$ grows, $\bar{D}_n$ grows, and $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_n)$ shrinks. Drag the slider to watch.

The toy theory

Three prime sentences with their intended ground-truth interpretation:

  • $p_1 := \quot{1+1=2}$ — provable from $\mathsf{PA}$, true in the standard model.
  • $p_2 := \quot{1+1=3}$ — its negation is provable from $\mathsf{PA}$.
  • $p_3 := \quot{\text{Goldbach's conjecture}}$ — open: neither $\mathsf{PA} \vdash p_3$ nor $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \lnot p_3$ is currently known.

That gives $2^3 = 8$ worlds — one per truth assignment $(p_1, p_2, p_3) \in \{0, 1\}^3$. Below is a hand-crafted deductive-process schedule that emits theorems through day 6.

    Why this matters for the criterion

    Two things to take away:

    1. The Logical Induction Criterion (§3.5–§3.6) grades a market $\mathbb{P}$ against the worlds in $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_n)$ on day $n$ — not against ground truth. A trader's plausible net worth is bounded only insofar as some world in $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_n)$ assigns it a small value. As more theorems arrive, fewer worlds get to be plausible, and the bound tightens.
    2. For sentences $\bar{D}_\infty$ never resolves (e.g., Goldbach above, if it really were independent of $\mathsf{PA}$), some world in $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_\infty)$ assigns $p_3 = 1$ and another assigns $p_3 = 0$. The market is free to set any price for $p_3$ between the two extremes; the criterion does not pin it down. This is exactly why a logical inductor can hover around $0.5$ on Goldbach and still satisfy the criterion.
    "Roughly speaking, we will mainly concern ourselves with the case where $\bar{D}$ eventually rules out all and only the worlds that are inconsistent with $\mathcal{T}$." — Garrabrant et al. (2016), §3.2

    What this widget is and isn't. The deductive-process schedule below is hand-crafted to stage one ruling-out per step for pedagogical clarity. The paper's canonical example is $\bar{D}_n =$ theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$ provable in at most $n$ characters — a deductive process whose growth rate is roughly exponential and whose first interesting theorems take many "days" to appear. The toy here is propositional only (no quantifiers, no axiom schemes), so $\mathrm{PC}$-checking reduces to truth-table evaluation; in the real LI setting, $\mathcal{S}$ is the language of $\mathsf{PA}$ and a "p.c. world" is any truth assignment that respects Boolean structure on Boolean combinations of sentences but can otherwise be wildly inconsistent (e.g., assign 1 to a sentence and 0 to one of its provable equivalents — Definition 3.2.3 only constrains Boolean combinations, not full first-order semantics). The scope-honest summary: this widget shows you how $\mathrm{PC}(\bar{D}_n)$ shrinks under propositional pressure; the real thing has more sentences and slower growth, but the same monotone-prune-on-finite-Boolean-evaluation logic.
