A market, a trader, and what it means to exploit

A market $\overline{\mathbb{P}} = (\mathbb{P}_1, \mathbb{P}_2, \ldots)$ is a computable sequence of pricings; on day $n$, $\mathbb{P}_n(\phi) \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ is the price of a share that pays \$1 if $\phi$ is true and \$0 otherwise. A deductive process $\overline{D}$ feeds the market a growing set $D_n$ of provable sentences. A trader $\overline{T}$ is a sequence of trading strategies — affine combinations of sentences with continuous coefficients — that buy and sell at the listed prices. The trader exploits the market if their plausible net worth $\{\, W(\sum_{i \le n} T_i) : n \in \mathbb{N}^+, W \in \mathrm{PC}(D_n)\,\}$ is bounded below but not above. The Logical Induction Criterion says: no efficiently-computable trader exploits a logical inductor.

The toy market

Three sentences, each priced constantly across all days (the simplest possible market that's still exploitable):

SentencePrice $\mathbb{P}_n$Deductive process resolves it on day…Truth in $\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{PA}$
$\varphi := \quot{1+1=2}$90¢$t_\varphi = 8$ ($\varphi \in D_n$ for $n \ge 8$)true (provable)
$\psi := \quot{1+1\ne 2}$5¢$t_\psi = 8$ ($\lnot\psi \in D_n$ for $n \ge 8$)false (refutable)
$\chi := \quot{\text{Goldbach's conjecture}}$98¢never (within $n \le 30$)undecided

All four sentences sit in $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}$. The deductive process eventually emits $\varphi$ and $\lnot\psi$ on day $t=8$; $\chi$ stays out of $D_n$ for the lifetime of this widget. (For the price trajectory before a logical inductor learns these sentences, see the convergence and provability-induction widgets later in the chapter.) Plausible worlds $\mathrm{PC}(D_n)$ are the propositionally-consistent truth assignments that agree with $D_n$. Before day 8 every plausible world is free to set $\varphi=0$ or $\varphi=1$; from day 8 on, every plausible world must agree $\varphi=1$ and $\psi=0$. $\chi$ is free at every $n$.

Pick a trader; read off whether they exploit

Each trader is a sequence $T_n$ of trading strategies for day $n$. Reading "$+1\,\varphi$" as "buy one share of $\varphi$ at the prevailing price", the strategy $T_n = \varphi - \mathbb{P}_n(\varphi)$ (the canonical "buy a $\varphi$-share, paying the listed price") is the simplest non-trivial strategy.

The dashed orange vertical line marks day $t=8$, when $\overline{D}$ resolves $\varphi$ and refutes $\psi$. After that day, every plausible world agrees on $\varphi$ and $\psi$, so the upper and lower bounds collapse on those terms. They stay separated for any holdings of $\chi$.

Why this market is exploitable at all

A market that prices a provable sentence at less than \$1 is leaving free money on the table once the deductive process catches up. Buying $\varphi$-shares at 90¢ before day 8 and holding them past day 8 nets 10¢ per share in every plausible world thereafter — a guaranteed positive return that grows linearly. Symmetrically, the trader who sells $\psi$-shares at 5¢ pockets 5¢ per share, and after day 8 every plausible world agrees those shares pay nothing, so the seller keeps the cash. Both are exploits.

The trader who buys $\chi$-shares at 98¢, by contrast, is making a bet. In some plausible worlds Goldbach is true and the bet pays off; in others it's false and the bet loses 98¢ each day. Their plausible worth has both an unbounded ceiling and an unbounded floor — not bounded below — and so by Definition 3.5.1 they do not exploit the market. This distinction is the whole point of the criterion: exploit ≠ "could possibly profit", but rather "guaranteed not to lose unboundedly while still possibly winning unboundedly".

"Roughly speaking, we can imagine that there is a person behind the market who acts as a market maker, obligated to buy and sell shares at the listed prices. We can imagine that anyone who sold a $\phi$-share is obligated to pay \$1 if and when $\overline{D}$ says $\phi$. Then, very roughly, a trader exploits the market if they are able to make unbounded returns off of a finite investment." — Garrabrant et al. (2016), §3.5

What this widget does not show

Two important limitations to flag, since either could mislead a careful reader.

  1. Continuous trading strategies. The paper requires trading strategies' coefficients to be continuous functions of the market history (Definition 3.4.4) so that the market-prices fixed-point construction in §5 works. The traders here all have constant coefficients (zeroth-degree polynomials in the price features), which is trivially continuous. The continuity machinery only bites when prices themselves depend on the trader (the $\chi := \quot{\text{I am true iff my price} < 0.5}$ paradox), which is the subject of the next widget.
  2. Polynomial-time computability. The criterion (Definition 3.6.1) restricts to efficiently-computable traders. Every strategy here is constant in $n$, so trivially polytime. Less efficient traders (e.g., the buy-low/sell-high trader of the convergence theorem, which would need to sweep the entire price history each day) sit just outside what this widget animates.

How the plot is computed. For each day $n=1,\ldots,30$, the widget evaluates the trader's holdings $H_n = \sum_{i \le n} T_i$ as an affine combination over $\{1, \varphi, \psi, \chi\}$ — i.e., a constant cash term plus per-sentence share counts. The plausible-worth bounds are then $\inf_{W \in \mathrm{PC}(D_n)} W(H_n)$ and $\sup_{W \in \mathrm{PC}(D_n)} W(H_n)$, computed by enumerating the $2^k$ truth assignments to the sentences left undecided by $D_n$. With three sentences this is $\le 8$ evaluations per day, well inside the bounds of an in-browser computation. The exploitation verdict applies the literal $n \le 30$ window — a trader that exploits "in the limit" but not by day 30 would be miscalled here, but for the four candidates listed the verdict on day 30 is the verdict in the limit.
