The continuity paradox: why traders can't be allowed to trade discontinuously

Define the sentence $\chi := \quot{\text{I am true iff my price is} < 50\text{¢}}$. By Gödel's diagonal lemma, such a sentence exists. Its payoff is then a function of its own price:

$\text{payout}(\chi) = \begin{cases} \$1 & \text{if } p_\chi < 0.5 \\ \$0 & \text{if } p_\chi \ge 0.5 \end{cases}$

A trader who can see the price wants to buy $\chi$ when it's cheap (below its $\$1$ payout) and sell when it's overpriced. The natural strategy: buy if $p < 0.5$, sell if $p \ge 0.5$. The market clears at a price where the trader chooses to do nothing — but does such a price exist?

$\varepsilon = 0$ is the discontinuous "step" trader the paper warns about. $\varepsilon > 0$ is a continuous trader insensitive to price shifts of size $\varepsilon$ around the threshold — an honest physical bound on price-resolution.

Why discontinuity breaks the market

A market-clearing price for $\chi$ is one where the trader has no incentive to trade — net demand $d(p) = 0$. With the discontinuous step strategy:

  • For any $p < 0.5$: $\chi$ pays $\$1$ but trades for less, so the trader buys ($d > 0$).
  • For any $p \ge 0.5$: $\chi$ pays $\$0$ but trades for at least $\$0.50$, so the trader sells ($d < 0$).

$d$ jumps from $+1$ to $-1$ at $p = 0.5$ without ever being zero. No clearing price exists. The market-maker has no way to set a price for $\chi$ that the trader will accept.

If we restrict traders to continuous demand functions — equivalently: the trader is insensitive to price shifts smaller than some $\varepsilon > 0$ — then $d(p)$ goes from positive at the left endpoint to negative at the right with no jumps. By the intermediate value theorem, $d$ has at least one zero on $[0, 1]$. A clearing price always exists.

"Continuity breaks the circularity, by ensuring that if there's a price where a trader buys $\chi$ and a price where they sell $\chi$, then there's a price in between where they neither buy nor sell. … The continuity constraint can be interpreted as saying that the trader has only finite-precision access to the market prices." — Garrabrant et al. (2016), §3.4

Why this matters for the rest of the paper

Continuity is doing two jobs at once:

  1. Mathematical: it lets the market-maker (§5.2) solve for prices via Brouwer's fixed-point theorem in $n$ dimensions, rather than getting stuck on a paradox.
  2. Philosophical: it lets the LIA dodge the liar paradox and its probabilistic cousin $\chi^{1/2}_n := \quot{\enc{\mathbb{P}}_n(\enc{\chi^{1/2}_n}) < 0.5}$ (§4.7). The diagonal lemma still produces such a sentence, but no continuous trader can exploit it indefinitely — its price ends up at exactly $0.5$ in the limit, which is the unique fixed point.

What the model is and isn't. The widget plots a single trader's net demand for $\chi$ as a function of $\chi$'s current price $p$, holding all other prices fixed. In the full §3.4 setting, traders depend on the entire price history $\Valuation_{\le n}$ across all sentences, and the market-maker (§5.2) clears prices for many sentences simultaneously — the relevant fixed-point theorem is then Brouwer's in $[0, 1]^k$, not the IVT in 1D. The 1D picture here captures the essential failure mode (a step function has no zero) and the essential rescue (continuity + IVT $\Rightarrow$ a zero exists), which is what the paper itself appeals to in its informal exposition. The smoothed strategy used here is $d(p) = \mathrm{clamp}((0.5 - p)/\varepsilon, -1, +1)$ when $\varepsilon > 0$, and the step function $d(p) = +1$ for $p < 0.5$, $d(p) = -1$ for $p \ge 0.5$ when $\varepsilon = 0$. The expressible-feature definition (§3.4) builds continuous strategies from rational arithmetic, $\max$, and safe reciprocation; a piecewise-linear ramp is a specific instance.
