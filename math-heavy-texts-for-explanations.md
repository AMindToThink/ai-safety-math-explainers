# Math-heavy texts ripe for interactive AI safety companions

The strongest single candidate is **Garrabrant et al.'s "Logical Induction" paper**, paired closely with **Watanabe's Singular Learning Theory** (Grey/Green books + DSLT corpus) and **Albarghouthi's *Introduction to Neural Network Verification***. Each has the rare combination Matthew should optimize for: a focused, math-heavy text that is currently genuinely hard to read, sits on the critical path of an active safety research program, and contains concepts whose natural pedagogy is *the learner doing something* — designing a trader, blowing up a singularity, or constructing an adversarial counterexample to a robustness certificate.

This report surveys 13 ranked candidates, distills 10 transferable techniques from existing interactive explainers, names three top picks with reasoning, flags texts that are already well-served, and identifies stretch candidates whose conversion would be transformatively valuable but probably requires more than 12 months.

A meta-observation across the survey: the safety field's pedagogical bottleneck is rarely "the text needs animated equations." It is almost always one of three things — (a) the **mathematical objects are intrinsically geometric or dynamic** but presented as static prose (SLT singularities, free-energy phase transitions, RLCT, attention circuits), (b) the **central learning task is "construct an example with property X"** but readers can only passively read the construction (logical induction traders, decision-theoretic counterexamples, verification certificates, do-calculus derivations), or (c) the **material is scattered across 20+ posts/papers** with drifting notation and no spine (ELK + heuristic arguments, Natural Abstractions, Anthropic's mech interp). Different texts cry out for different pedagogical interventions, and choosing the right text means choosing which intervention you can execute best.

## Ranked candidate texts

### 1. Logical Induction — Garrabrant, Benson-Tilsen, Critch, Soares, Taylor (2016)

Single ~100-page arXiv paper (1609.03543). Foundational result on reasoning under logical/computational uncertainty; underpins later work on bounded rationality, Vingean reflection, embedded decision theory, and Demski's Radical Probabilism. **Math prerequisites are surprisingly tractable**: probability, basic mathematical logic and Peano arithmetic, polynomial-time computability, martingale/Dutch-book arguments, light real analysis — well within Matthew's stated background.

The paper is currently inaccessible because it stacks ~25 lemmas on an idiosyncratic trading-formula syntax, the actual algorithm (LIA) is buried in §5.4, and the central object — a *market of polynomial-time traders* — is described abstractly even though the metaphor is the most pedagogically useful one in all of agent foundations. Demski's "intuitive guide" and Adam Scherlis's "Logical Induction for Software Engineers" (with Python repo) cover the surface; **no interactive companion exists, and the natural learner-action — *be a trader who exploits a market* — is built into the formalism**.

The gamification opportunities are almost embarrassingly direct. A "Break the inductor" sandbox where the learner writes a polynomial-time trader in a code editor and watches whether it earns unbounded value against the inductor running on a chosen deductive process. A market-maker visualizer animating the price trajectory of any sentence φ as theorems stream in, with pause/inject. A self-reference puzzle where the learner constructs Gödel/liar-style sentences and watches prices approach the Theorem 4.6.2 fixed point. A π-digit prediction game embodying the paper's canonical example. A calibration/coherence dashboard showing the 17+ desiderata being satisfied in real time. Scope is roughly 12–18 chapters covering motivation, the trading framework, the LIC, LIA, computability, basic properties, statistical learning, self-trust, limit coherence, and downstream work (Asymptotic LIDT, untrollable mathematician). **A near-perfect 6–12 month fit**.

### 2. Watanabe's Singular Learning Theory — *Algebraic Geometry and Statistical Learning Theory* (2009) and *Mathematical Theory of Bayesian Statistics* (2018)

Together with the Timaeus/DevInterp ecosystem (Carroll's DSLT 0–4, Furman's exercises, Hoogland's free-energy posts, the `devinterp` Python library, metauni seminars), the "Grey Book" and "Green Book" form the mathematical spine of **the most active formal-theory program in alignment**: developmental interpretability, Local Learning Coefficient (LLC) estimation, Bayesian phase transitions, free-energy explanations of grokking, and SLT-native circuit discovery. Anthropic, DeepMind, and OpenAI have all engaged with this agenda; Timaeus has briefed all three.

Prerequisites are **the steepest on this list**: real and complex analysis, measure-theoretic probability, differential geometry, commutative algebra and algebraic geometry (varieties, ideals, blow-ups, resolution of singularities), Schwartz distributions, and empirical processes. Watanabe writes as a mathematician for mathematicians: ~250 pages of dense theorem-proof structure, terse compressed background chapters, almost no diagrams, and a climactic free-energy formula that depends on a stack of results no one can hold in their head simultaneously.

But **almost every chapter contains concepts that are intrinsically geometric and currently presented as prose** — exactly the highest-leverage situation for visualization. An interactive widget where you draw a 2D variety with a singularity (V(x²−y³)) and watch a blow-up disentangle it into normal-crossing form. An RLCT calculator that takes a polynomial loss landscape and computes λ via Newton polyhedra. A free-energy formula visualizer with a slider over n showing how phases with different (λ, m) dominate at different sample sizes. A singular-vs-regular Fisher matrix explorer on a 1-hidden-unit ReLU net. A phase-transition simulator reproducing DSLT4 curves live. WBIC vs. BIC widgets for student-trained models.

Scope is large but tractable if the project covers Chapters 1, 4, 6, 7 of the Grey Book in depth (skipping the algebraic-geometry crash course, which is the boring compression) plus Green Book WAIC/WBIC machinery and DevInterp case studies — roughly 12–18 chapters of explainer pages. **The community of buyers (MATS scholars, Timaeus, ARENA participants) is large, and they would almost certainly adopt and amplify a unified interactive treatment.**

### 3. Aws Albarghouthi — *Introduction to Neural Network Verification* (2021)

Free at verifieddeeplearning.com. The textbook for formal NN verification: robustness certificates, Reluplex/Marabou, abstract interpretation (DeepPoly, CROWN, α,β-CROWN), abstract training. Connects directly to **Davidad's Safeguarded AI program, Lyapunov-stability for learned controllers, and "Guaranteed Safe AI"** writ large. ~150 pages, three parts — perfect length.

Prerequisites: linear algebra, basic logic and SAT/SMT, convex analysis, abstract interpretation theory. The current inaccessibility is twofold: abstract interpretation is hard to visualize beyond toy examples, and the chain LP-relaxation → dual bounds → branch-and-bound is rarely shown end-to-end with intuition. Tutorials exist for α,β-CROWN/auto_LiRPA, but no structured curriculum.

The gamification fit may be **the cleanest on this list** because verification is *natively adversarial*. A verification game presents the learner with a 2-layer ReLU net and a spec ("for all x in this ε-ball, output class is dog") and asks them to either construct an adversarial counterexample or construct a CROWN-style linear bound proving robustness — the site grades both. An abstract-interpretation visualizer shows zonotopes/polyhedra propagating through ReLU layers as the learner picks the abstract domain. A branch-and-bound interactive lets the learner pick splitting heuristics and race α,β-CROWN's defaults on a leaderboard. Spec-writing exercises bridge the under-taught "spec gap" — translating an English safety property into a ∀∃ statement on a network. A Lyapunov-NN game wraps verification of learned controllers. Verification is **already a competition** (VNN-COMP); wrapping the curriculum around live competition tooling is natural.

### 4. Cartesian Frames — Scott Garrabrant (2020)

12-post sequence on the Alignment Forum (Oct–Nov 2020) plus a Garrabrant–Heymann arXiv paper (2109.10996). A categorical formalism for the agent/environment Cartesian boundary problem from Embedded Agency, providing language for subagents, multi-agency, observability, controllability, and time-as-coarsening.

**This is the cleanest, most bounded scope on the list.** Prerequisites are modest: discrete math, basic category theory (functors, products, biextensional equivalence), Boolean lattices, a little game-theoretic intuition, matrix-style notation. The accessibility problem is structural: the sequence has ~80 distinct definitions/lemmas, idiosyncratic Garrabrant matrix notation appearing nowhere else, lots of small lemmas with no narrative examples, and most readers stop at post 3–4 because the payoff is back-loaded.

Specific interactive ideas: a frame-builder widget where the learner picks A, E, W and the interaction function and the system computes ensurables, controllables, observables, and biextensional equivalence class; a "carve up the world" puzzle where, given a fixed world W, the learner finds different frames decomposing it; a subagent-finder visualizing how a sub-frame embeds via the categorical operations; a time-as-partition explorer making post 12's notion interactive ("controllables decrease, observables increase" emerges as you coarsen); a composition arena for tensor product / par. **This could plausibly fit in 6 months rather than 12** — making it the safest pick if Matthew wants to ship.

### 5. Anthropic Mechanistic Interpretability cluster — *A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits* (2021) + *Toy Models of Superposition* (2022) + follow-ups

Elhage et al.'s framework paper plus TMS plus *Towards Monosemanticity*, *Scaling Monosemanticity*, *Privileged Bases*, sparse crosscoders, and the 2025 Haiku attribution graphs. Foundational empirical interpretability paradigm; **the most-cited single corpus in modern alignment research**.

Prerequisites: heavy linear algebra (tensor products, low-rank decompositions, QK/OV factorizations), probability, optimization, sparse coding. The framework paper is dense matrix-tensor algebra with unusual notation; readers regularly report 4–10 hours of careful re-reading before it clicks. Toy Models is the most accessible entry point and already has interactive figures. The deeper inaccessibility is structural: there is **no textbook spine** connecting the 30+ Transformer Circuits papers — definitions of "feature," "circuit," "superposition," "polysemanticity," "attribution graph" drift across papers, notation differs from the earlier Distill Circuits Thread, and Olah explicitly publishes via "research diaries" rather than building a unified reference.

Concrete interactive opportunities: a QK/OV-circuit decomposer loading a small attention-only transformer and rendering both projections live; a path-expansion visualizer scrubbing residual-stream paths; an induction-head formation animation showing K-composition emerging as a phase change; a TMS phase-diagram explorer animating transitions across (sparsity × importance) space; a polytope-identifier gallery; SAE feature atlases; attribution-graph crawlers for small models. **The scope is the largest of any candidate** — easily 600+ pages textbook equivalent if consolidated through 2026 — which makes a tightly scoped picking of (Math Framework + TMS + induction heads + one SAE paper) a more realistic 12-month target than full coverage. Caveat: ARENA Chapter 1 and Neel Nanda's TransformerLens tutorials already cover much of this terrain at tutorial depth, so differentiation requires going deeper into the math the tutorials skip.

### 6. Agarwal, Jiang, Kakade, Sun — *Reinforcement Learning: Theory and Algorithms* (AJKS, 2022, working draft)

Free at rltheorybook.github.io. The canonical theoretical reference under all modern RLHF/PPO/GRPO/DPO work — sample complexity of policy optimization, NPG/TRPO convergence, function approximation lower bounds, offline RL with coverage, exploration. **Matthew's existing RL-theory expertise positions him uniquely well to author this one.**

Prerequisites are substantial: measure-theoretic probability, linear algebra, convex analysis, concentration inequalities, comfort with regret/PAC analysis. Currently inaccessible because pure-prose proofs have almost no figures, theorem-lemma-proof rhythm offers little intuition, and assumptions like concentrability, Bellman-completeness, and low-rank MDPs are abstract conditions students rarely visualize.

Interactive ideas: an MDP "concentrability lab" where the learner picks behavior policy μ and target π, and the site visualizes distribution-mismatch ratio C and watches FQI/batch RL bounds tighten/loosen live; an NPG/TRPO/PPO trust-region visualizer in 2D log-policy space with a KL-ball overlay; a linear-MDP feature playground computing effective dimension and plotting LSVI regret bound vs. simulation; a **lower-bound adversary game** where the learner constructs an MDP that defeats a given algorithm's regret bound, with the site reporting whether they've matched the lower bound; coverage/exploration bonus sliders. The **headline opportunity is an "RLHF/DPO missing chapter"** — extending AJKS's offline-RL machinery to derive DPO/GRPO sample complexity, where Matthew's empirical work is directly relevant. Roughly 250 pages, 12 chapters, well-sized for the timeline.

### 7. Peters, Janzing & Schölkopf — *Elements of Causal Inference* (2017)

MIT Press, free open-access PDF. Ties to causal-representation learning, robustness to distribution shift, OOD generalization, interpretability via interventions, and **causal incentives frameworks for reward hacking analysis** (Everitt et al.). Underexploited connection: confounded RLHF preference data is a textbook causal inference problem.

Prerequisites are gentle: probability, conditional independence, basic linear algebra, light graph theory. Currently inaccessible because do-calculus, SCMs, and identifiability proofs are presented at high abstraction; bivariate identifiability via ANM is famously unintuitive; Markov equivalence classes are hard to visualize at scale.

The **headline interactive opportunity is a do-calculus puzzle game** — Pearl himself has lamented for a decade that no one has built one. Given a DAG and a target query P(Y | do(X)), the learner applies rules 1–3 step-by-step, the system checks each rewrite and offers hints, in a Lean-style puzzle UI. An SCM sandbox lets you drag-build a structural causal model and toggle observe/intervene/counterfactual to see Pearl's three rungs. A bivariate-identifiability widget animates ANM identifiability succeeding or failing as you slide noise distributions and nonlinearities. A causal-discovery race shows PC vs. GES vs. LiNGAM on user-drawn DAGs. A **confounded-RLHF demo** simulates a preference-data SCM where annotator competence confounds style and correctness, and shows how a causal correction repairs the reward model. Scope is 288 pages; tractable.

### 8. Infra-Bayesianism — Vanessa Kosoy & Alex Appel (2020–present)

The Infra-Bayesianism sequence on the Alignment Forum, plus *Infra-Bayesian Physicalism* and follow-ups, plus Kosoy's Learning-Theoretic Agenda. Currently the most active formal alignment research program with a coherent agenda; answers prior-misspecification/nonrealizability/grain-of-truth, gives a UDT-flavored decision theory, and underwrites Kosoy's *Physicalist Superimitation* alignment proposal.

Prerequisites are **the heaviest on the list**: measure theory, functional and convex analysis on spaces of measures, Choquet integration, imprecise probability, RL regret-bound theory, some category theory, topology of compact Hausdorff spaces. Currently inaccessible in a deep way: dense LaTeX-on-Forum, non-standard notation, theorems with six-line preconditions, ~10 new objects (a-measures, sa-measures, infradistributions, infrakernels) introduced before any worked examples. Multiple distillations exist (Shimi's Unwrapped, Kirchner's Elementary intro, Jan/Joshc's posts) but **each only covers the first 1–2 posts** — the technical core (IBP, monotonicity principle, regret bounds, IBP quantum agreement) has no accessible companion.

Interactive opportunities: a credal-set widget letting you drag-and-drop a convex set of probability distributions and watch maximin EU computation in real time; a Dutch-book attacker game where the learner is "Omega" trying to Dutch-book a Bayesian agent (loses) vs. an infra-Bayesian agent (refuses); a Newcomb-variants playground; a hypothesis-class builder that shows IB regret converging while Bayesian regret diverges on non-realizable classes; a monotonicity-principle explorer. **This is the candidate the agent-foundations community most wants and is least equipped to consume — but it's a stretch project (see Stretch section).**

### 9. Risks from Learned Optimization + Conditioning Predictive Models + Scheming AIs (Hubinger et al., Carlsmith)

The mesa-optimization sequence (1906.01820), the conditioning-predictive-models sequence (2302.00805), and Carlsmith's *Scheming AIs* (2311.08379) — together, **the foundational vocabulary of inner alignment used throughout the field**.

Prerequisites are moderate: Bayesian decision theory, basic optimization, computational complexity, learning theory. Less mathematically dense than ARC/SLT but conceptually intricate. The accessibility problem is the parallel-sequences-with-drifting-vocabulary issue: three sequences (~40, 70, 130 pages) that cross-reference but never settle on canonical notation; Scheming AIs is a 6-hour audio narration plus dense PDF.

Interactive ideas: a mesa-optimizer toy taxonomy as a clickable tree of (internal/corrigible/deceptive aligned) with worked maze gridworlds; a **counting-argument calculator** parametrizing the Scheming AIs §4 inductive-bias argument and showing "how many goals are schemer-compatible"; speed-vs-simplicity priors visualizers; a conditioning sandbox on a tiny LM where users add observations and see inferred world distributions shift. Lower mathematical content than other candidates, but **highest pedagogical impact per reader** because every alignment researcher reads these.

### 10. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning — Albrecht, Christianos & Schäfer (MIT, 2024)

Free at marl-book.com with codebase. Covers AI debate (Irving/Christiano/Amodei, plus Brown-Cohen/Irving/Piliouras 2023 doubly-efficient debate), CIRL, cooperative AI, bargaining/conflict in multi-AI ecosystems (ARCHES), self-play, emergent collusion. **Uniquely well-aligned with present-day "many AIs interacting" safety concerns**.

Prerequisites: game theory (Nash, correlated, Stackelberg), MDPs/POMDPs, deep RL, fixed-point analysis. Inaccessibility: the solution-concept zoo is confusing without visualization; CTDE and value-decomposition techniques are described algorithmically without showing the game-theoretic implications; debate/CIRL papers cited at chapter end need integrated treatment.

Multi-agent settings are **intrinsically gameable**: equilibrium playgrounds for 2×2–5×5 games with Nash/correlated/coarse-correlated computed and dynamics (fictitious play, no-regret, replicator) animated; a debate-game simulator implementing the Irving et al. protocol with the learner playing judge or debater; a CIRL teaching/learning sandbox; self-play arenas with PPO/PSRO toggles and exploitability gauges; an emergent-collusion lab; a mechanism-design widget. ~400 pages with a working codebase, sizable but the codebase reduces dev cost.

### 11. ELK + Heuristic Arguments cluster (Christiano, Cotra, Xu, Neyman, ARC)

Eliciting Latent Knowledge (~100-page Google Doc), *Formalizing the Presumption of Independence* (arXiv:2211.06738), heuristic-arguments / surprise-accounting / no-coincidence-principle posts. **Widely viewed as the cleanest formalization of the deep alignment problem**; ARC's bet on a mathematical foundation for mech interp.

Prerequisites: probability, information theory, complexity theory (BPP, advice strings), measure theory, cumulant expansions. The original ELK report is mostly conceptual but the heuristic-arguments line is heavy. Inaccessibility is severe and structural: ELK is a 100+ page Google Doc with ~30 (proposal, counterexample) pairs in dialogue format that resists linear reading; the heuristic-arguments material is split across the 2022 paper, 2024 surprise-accounting post, 2025 no-coincidence post, and the *Steelmanning* reflection — no textbook, no exercises, no unified notation.

Interactive ideas: an ELK strategy/counterexample explorer as a graph UI walking through the (proposal, counterexample) pairs with diagrams of "human Bayes net vs. predictor Bayes net"; a cumulant-propagation simulator on small Boolean circuits; a **max-of-k heuristic-explanation walkthrough** as an interactive proof companion; a no-coincidence-conjecture verifier sandbox; a surprise-accounting calculator.

### 12. Power-Seeking Theorems — Alex Turner et al. (2019–2022)

NeurIPS 2021 paper, NeurIPS 2022 follow-up, Turner's PhD thesis (arXiv:2206.11831), Reframing Impact sequence. **The only rigorous formal theory we have of instrumental convergence** — that most reward functions in an MDP induce optimal policies that resist shutdown. Compact and contested: a 2025 Reflective Altruism critique argues the conclusions don't quite match the theorems, exactly the kind of clarification an interactive companion could deliver.

Prerequisites: MDP theory, group theory (environment symmetries/orbits), measure theory over reward distributions, average-reward/Blackwell-optimality, combinatorics. The 2021 paper has gone through 10 revisions with shifting notation; the thesis is 200+ pages. Interactive opportunities: an MDP gridworld with orbit visualization (user designs an environment, sees the symmetry group acting on reward functions, watches POWER computed at each state); a "larger sets of recurrent state distributions" animation; counterfactual environment editing with shutdown actions added/removed; a Pac-Man example. ~150 pages textbook equivalent, ~20–25 modules — a tight, focused project.

### 13. Computational Mechanics for Transformers — Shai, Riechers, Simplex (2024)

Belief-state geometry paper (arXiv:2405.15943). Possibly the cleanest connection between formal information theory (epsilon-machines, Crutchfield-style computational mechanics) and what transformers actually represent — a principled answer to "what is a world model?" via the linearly-encoded mixed-state simplex.

Prerequisites: computational mechanics, hidden Markov models, fractal geometry, linear algebra on belief simplices, information theory. The arXiv paper is short and assumes computational-mechanics background most ML researchers don't have; Crutchfield's epsilon-machine literature is its own physics-flavored research line; LW posts and AXRP Ep 36 are the only intermediates. **Highest visualization-payoff-per-page on the list** — the belief-state simplex literally forms fractals you can render. Interactive ideas: a fractal generator picking an HMM (Mess3) and watching the mixed-state presentation form; live training visualizations showing the residual stream geometry converge; epsilon-machine builders. ~100–150 pages, ~15 modules — small, focused, beautiful.

## Inspirational interactive explainers and what to steal

Across 25 reviewed sources, ten transferable techniques recurred and matter most for math-heavy AI safety material.

**Single-toy-model elaborated through a chapter**, exemplified by **Ciechanowski's articles** (ciechanow.ski) and **Immersive Linear Algebra**, is the cornerstone pattern. Pick one minimal AI-safety-relevant system per chapter — a 2-token transformer, 3-state MDP, 2-player game, 1-hidden-unit ReLU net — and reuse it across every widget, progressively exposing more parameters. Beats a parade of one-off examples. Ciechanowski's signature "start with a wooden box, earn complexity" with one new variable per widget is the gold standard.

**Linked views driven by one control**, from **Distill's "Why Momentum Really Works"** and **Seeing Theory**, has one slider drive 2–3 coordinated visualizations: parameter space + function space + loss landscape, or prior + likelihood + posterior. Forces the reader to feel equivalences between representations. Distill articles also demonstrate the value of **interpretability methods as composable primitives** that the reader can stack — extremely apt for SLT and mech interp chapters.

**Bret Victor's Ladder of Abstraction** is the chapter-level template: concrete state → control time → abstract over time → abstract over a parameter → always provide a hover-based step-down to a concrete instance. His **Tangle.js** library powers inline scrubbable numbers ("if we train for [10000] steps with [β=0.9]…"), trivial to add and dramatically improving engagement.

**Place-Your-Bets / forced prediction**, from **Nicky Case's explorables** and **Brilliant.org**, requires a click before revealing any answer. This single change converts passive reading into active learning. Brilliant validates the pattern at scale; Nicky Case has documented it explicitly in "How I Make Explorable Explanations."

**End every chapter with a Sandbox**, from **Earth: A Primer**, **Parable of the Polygons**, **Evolution of Trust**, and **LOOPY**. A free-play mode using the chapter's tools where the reader poses their own questions — the "going beyond the author" pattern. For AI safety: a "build-your-own-mesa-optimizer" sandbox after the inner alignment chapter, a "tweak-an-RLHF-reward-model" playground after the RLHF chapter.

**The mnemonic medium with expert-authored SR prompts inline**, from **Quantum Country** and **Andy Matuschak's Orbit**. Definitions and theorem statements embedded as flashcards scheduled by email. **Critical for AI safety material because the field has dense jargon** (corrigibility, mesa-optimization, deceptive alignment, free energy, λ, RLCT) where forgetting blocks all subsequent reading. Orbit (BSD-licensed, embeddable iframes) is purpose-built for this and ships today.

**Construct-the-object games**, from **Lean's Natural Number Game** and the broader **Lean Game Server**. The mathematical content (proofs) *is* the gameplay, not a wrapper. Browser-only, no install. For AI safety, this means wrapping any theorem-style content as Lean game levels — proving correctness of a verification condition, constructing counterexamples to alignment guarantees, deriving regret bounds. Even non-formal "construct an example with property X" puzzles can use this UI. **For Logical Induction specifically, the trader-construction sandbox is exactly this pattern.**

**Embodied agent-based simulations of multi-agent failure modes**, from **Polygons**, **Evolution of Trust**, and **LOOPY**. For game-theoretic and emergence arguments — collusion, mesa-optimizers, gradient hacking — let the reader place agents, edit payoffs, and watch population-level pathology arise. Embodied causation beats prose for emergence.

**Editable code embedded in prose**, from **probmods.org**, **Observable**, **agentmodels.org**, and **Nielsen's *Neural Networks and Deep Learning***. Pyodide for Python in browser, WebPPL for probabilistic programs, Observable Framework for rapid authoring. **agentmodels.org especially relevant** — it's a free working interactive book about modeling rational/bounded agents that already covers many AI-safety-adjacent concepts and is forkable.

**Bite-sized single-concept widgets**, from **Setosa.io** and **Red Blob Games**. Not every interaction needs to be elaborate. A two-paragraph page with one draggable element is often the best unit — cheap to build, easy to embed inline, easy to fork. Reserve elaborate Ciechanowski-style widgets for the 5–10 "ah-ha" moments per chapter.

**Implementation stack** to consider: D3.js or Observable Framework for visualizations; Manim Community Edition for pre-rendered transformation clips; Andy Matuschak's Orbit for spaced repetition; Lean 4 Game Server for theorem-proving exercises; Pyodide or WebPPL for editable code; LOOPY (forkable, MIT-licensed) for causal/system modeling. All of these ship permissive licenses.

## Three top picks

**Pick #1: Logical Induction.** This is the single best fit. The text is bounded (one ~100-page paper), the prerequisites match Matthew's background almost exactly, and **the natural learner-action — design a polynomial-time trader who exploits the market — is built into the formalism** in a way that no other candidate matches. Demski and Scherlis have already done the prose distillation; what remains is precisely the interactive layer Matthew would build. Six to twelve months gives plausible coverage of the LIC, LIA, calibration, self-trust, limit coherence, and one or two follow-on chapters on logical-inductor decision theory. The companion site would become canonical — there is currently no interactive treatment, and the agent-foundations community has been waiting for one for a decade.

**Pick #2: Singular Learning Theory.** This is the highest-leverage *research-relevance* pick. Timaeus/DevInterp is a fast-growing, well-funded program with active uptake at Anthropic, DeepMind, and OpenAI; the math is intrinsically geometric (singularities, blow-ups, free-energy phase transitions, fractal posteriors) and currently presented as nearly diagram-free monograph prose; Carroll's DSLT and Furman's exercises provide a partial outline; the `devinterp` library gives backend infrastructure. The downside is a steep prerequisite ramp (algebraic geometry, measure theory) that may force tight scope-cutting — the recommendation is to cover the Grey Book's Chapters 1, 4, 6, 7 plus Green Book WAIC/WBIC plus 2–3 DevInterp case studies, not the full text. **Choose this one if Matthew prefers maximum impact in an active research community over scope safety.**

**Pick #3: Albarghouthi's *Introduction to Neural Network Verification*.** This is the safest pick that still has world-class gamification potential. Length is ideal (~150 pages), prerequisites are CS-friendly, the verification-vs-attack adversarial framing is *natively* a game, the α,β-CROWN/auto_LiRPA/Marabou/VNN-COMP infrastructure is mature, and the "Guaranteed Safe AI" agenda is rapidly growing in influence (Davidad's £59M ARIA program, Tegmark, etc.). The signature feature would be a verification game where the learner must either construct an adversarial counterexample or construct a CROWN-style linear bound proving robustness, with the site grading both — this maps perfectly to the Lean-game pattern but with a more familiar object (a network) and a more practically valuable skill (specification writing for safety).

A near-miss honorable mention is **Cartesian Frames**, which has the cleanest bounded scope of any candidate (12 posts, ~12 chapters, possibly 6 months) and excellent puzzle-style gamification potential. The reason it doesn't crack the top three: the research program around it has cooled somewhat compared to SLT, IB, and Wentworth's Natural Abstractions, so the audience is narrower.

## Already well-served — do not pick

**Sutton & Barto's *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*** has Shangtong Zhang's PyTorch repo, Denny Britz's notebooks, the Coursera specialization, Sutton's slides, and a complete educational ecosystem — diminishing returns. **Boyd & Vandenberghe's *Convex Optimization*** has Stanford EE364a's free videos, slides, exercises, and CVX tooling, arguably the gold-standard existing companion. **Cover & Thomas** and **MacKay's *Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms*** both have huge online followings, free PDFs, multiple video courses, and several interactive companions. **Mathematics in Lean / Theorem Proving in Lean 4 / Software Foundations** are *already* maximally interactive — the text *is* a Lean/Coq script you execute. **The illustrated Embedded Agency** by Garrabrant and Demski is the most pedagogically polished document in agent foundations; better used as a *navigation hub linking to companions for the deeper formal works* than as a conversion target itself. **Risks from Learned Optimization** is well-served by Rob Miles videos, AF reviews, and reading groups, and is too low-on-formal-math for Matthew's stated profile. **Mohri/Rostamizadeh/Talwalkar** and **Shalev-Shwartz/Ben-David** are well-pedagogized standard references where the marginal AI-safety relevance is moderate. **Hardt & Recht's *Patterns, Predictions, and Actions*** is already free, accessible, and not particularly math-heavy by the criteria here.

## Stretch candidates

**Infra-Bayesianism** would be transformatively valuable if accessible — it's the single most-requested distillation in agent foundations, and Kosoy's program is the most active formal alignment research line — but the prerequisite ramp (Choquet integration, convex analysis on measure spaces) is severe, the corpus is genuinely 25–30+ modules, and Kosoy continues publishing follow-up work that would shift the target during a 12-month build. Plausible only if Matthew is willing to commit 12+ months and accept that he'll be learning the math as he writes.

**Roberts, Yaida & Hanin's *Principles of Deep Learning Theory*** (470 pages, physics-flavored 1/n-expansions, Feynman-diagram-style calculations) and **Wainwright's *High-Dimensional Statistics*** (600+ pages, encyclopedic) are both important and inaccessible but **too long for one student in 12 months** — would require brutal scope-cutting (e.g., "Wainwright Ch. 1–6 + 13" or "Roberts criticality + finite-width corrections only").

**John Wentworth's Natural Abstractions / Selection Theorems corpus** (50+ posts, 300–500 pages textbook equivalent) is the largest unconsolidated single-author corpus in alignment and the community has been begging for distillation. The Chan–Jenner–Bushnaq formalization of the Telephone Theorem and gKPD provides a partial scaffold. The downsides: Wentworth has acknowledged updating in real-time on the meaning of his own theorems, results are stated semi-formally, and Soares has published a critical review questioning whether the results say what is claimed. Stretch because the project would partly be original mathematical consolidation, not just pedagogical distillation.

**A consolidated Anthropic Mechanistic Interpretability monograph** unifying the 30+ Transformer Circuits papers would be the single largest "should be a textbook" target in the field — but consolidation across drifting notation is a research project in its own right and ARENA Chapter 1 already covers significant ground at tutorial depth.

**Davidad's Open Agency Architecture / Safeguarded AI**: aspirational, the math is partly not yet written down, and the underlying machinery (infra-Bayesianism + categorical composition + formal verification) compounds three other stretch candidates. Wait until the ARIA TA1 contracts produce a unified write-up.

## Closing observation

The convergent signal across all five research streams is that **the safety field's pedagogical bottleneck is not "explain the math better"** — it's "let the reader *construct* the mathematical object the text discusses." Logical Induction's traders, SLT's blow-ups, verification's certificates, do-calculus derivations, decision-theoretic counterexamples, mesa-optimizer designs, and infra-Bayesian credal sets are all fundamentally *constructive* objects whose pedagogy peaks when the learner builds one. The Lean Game Server pattern, Brilliant's forced predictions, Project Euler's binary verification, Human Resource Machine's dual scoring, and Nicky Case's sandboxes all converge on the same insight from different directions: build the mathematical object, get graded, iterate. **Choosing among the top three picks reduces to choosing which constructive task Matthew wants to teach: design a trader (Logical Induction), find a singularity and resolve it (SLT), or attack-or-defend a network (Verification).** Any of the three would produce something the AI safety community currently lacks and would adopt.
